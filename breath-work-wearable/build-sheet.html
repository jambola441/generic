Breath Puck Build Sheet

Everything needed to go from zero hardware experience to a working breathwork wearable on your shirt: parts with links and prices, the wiring, the shells to print, the firmware, the phone side, and the order to do it in.

What you are building

One unit, two discs, three places to wear it

Two 36 mm discs joined by a short grosgrain ribbon. The ribbon drapes over a fabric edge and carries the weight; the magnets in each disc clamp through the fabric and keep it still. The outer disc holds the radio, the motion sensor and the battery. The inner disc sits against skin and holds the vibration motor, so you feel the pacing where it matters.

v0 goal (this build)

A puck you can wear that streams chest motion to your phone over Bluetooth and plays a slow haptic swell to pace breathing. Off-the-shelf boards, printed shells, ten soldered wires. Done when a friend can follow a five-minute paced session with their eyes closed and the app's breath count matches a manual count.

What v0 deliberately skips

Custom circuit board, light ring, wideband haptic motor, waterproofing, charging dock, certification. Each is listed under path to a real product. None is needed to learn whether people want this.

Zero-hardware parallel track. Your phone has the same kind of accelerometer. Lay it flat on your chest and you can build and tune the whole breath-detection algorithm in the app before a single part arrives. Do that during the shipping week.

Mechanical design

Two cups, two lids, one ribbon

v0 is 36 mm across because the two off-the-shelf boards need a 32.4 mm cavity. The custom board in v1 brings it to 32 mm. Thicknesses are driven by the stack inside each cup.

Assembly notes

  1. Magnet polarity first. Stack all four D81 magnets, mark the top face of each with a dot, then separate. In each disc, glue two magnets dot-down into the cup floor near the rim, opposite each other. Put the outer disc's magnets dot-up instead. Test attraction through a t-shirt before any glue cures.
  2. Motor coupling. Superglue the LRA flat against the inside of the inner cup's floor (that floor becomes the skin wall). A loose motor feels like a cheap buzzer; a rigid one feels like a phone.
  3. Battery. Kapton over the XIAO's underside, battery on top of the tape, wires to the BAT pads. Confirm polarity with the multimeter before soldering. Red is positive.
  4. Ribbon. Cut 70 mm. Lay the four wires along the centre, fold the ribbon lengthwise and tack with Kapton, then E6000 each end into a rim notch. Leave 25 mm of ribbon exposed between the cups.
  5. Lids. Dry-fit everything and run the firmware before gluing lids on. A dab of E6000 at three points so you can pry it open later.
Wiring schematic

Ten wires

Four cross the ribbon (power, ground, two I²C lines). Two join the motor to its driver inside the inner disc. Two join the battery to the XIAO. The motion sensor is already on the XIAO, so there is nothing to wire for it.

Haptic design

A swell, not a buzz

Notifications live around 200 to 250 Hz, where skin is most sensitive and the feeling is alerting. Calming devices sit lower and change slowly. In v0 the carrier is fixed at the motor's 170 Hz resonance; what you shape is the amplitude envelope over seconds. In v1 a wideband actuator lets you lower the carrier toward 60 to 120 Hz as well.

Patterns to ship in v0

Coherent
5.5 bpm, equal in and out, continuous swell (above)
Box 4-4-4-4
swell in, hold at 35%, fade out, silence; a soft 60 ms tick at each phase change
4-7-8
short swell, long low hold, long fade; wind-down for sleep
Follow me
no pacing; haptic mirrors the user's own detected breath so they feel it. Surprisingly powerful

Firmware

Arduino, two libraries, about 150 lines

Board package: Seeed's non-mbed Seeed nRF52 Boards (it bundles Adafruit's Bluefruit BLE stack). Libraries: Seeed LSM6DS3, Adafruit_DRV2605. The puck does two things on timers and nothing else.

#include <LSM6DS3.h>
#include <Wire.h>
#include <Adafruit_DRV2605.h>
#include <bluefruit.h>

LSM6DS3 imu(I2C_MODE, 0x6A);           // on the XIAO's internal bus
Adafruit_DRV2605 drv;                  // on Wire (D4/D5), address 0x5A

BLEService        svc  ("7a0b0001-6f3c-4d2e-9b1a-0b7e47b5c001");
BLECharacteristic imuC ("7a0b0002-6f3c-4d2e-9b1a-0b7e47b5c001");  // notify: int16 ax,ay,az + uint16 seq
BLECharacteristic hapC ("7a0b0003-6f3c-4d2e-9b1a-0b7e47b5c001");  // write: uint8 amplitude 0..127, or a pattern struct
BLEBas            bas;                 // standard battery service

volatile uint8_t amp = 0;

void onHaptic(uint16_t, BLECharacteristic*, uint8_t* d, uint16_t n) { if (n) amp = d[0] & 0x7F; }

void setup() {
  imu.settings.accelSampleRate = 26;   // Hz; breathing is 0.1–0.5 Hz, 26 is plenty
  imu.settings.accelRange = 2;         // ±2 g
  imu.settings.gyroEnabled = 0;        // save power
  imu.begin();

  drv.begin();
  drv.useLRA();
  drv.writeRegister8(DRV2605_REG_RATEDV, 0x3E);   // ≈2.5 Vrms for the VLV101040A; tune after auto-cal
  drv.writeRegister8(DRV2605_REG_CLAMPV, 0x89);   // overdrive clamp
  drv.setMode(0x07); drv.go(); delay(1200);       // one-time auto-calibration, then
  drv.setMode(DRV2605_MODE_REALTIME);             // real-time playback: amplitude from a register

  Bluefruit.begin(); Bluefruit.setName("BreathPuck");
  svc.begin();
  imuC.setProperties(CHR_PROPS_NOTIFY); imuC.setFixedLen(8); imuC.begin();
  hapC.setProperties(CHR_PROPS_WRITE | CHR_PROPS_WRITE_WO_RESP); hapC.setWriteCallback(onHaptic); hapC.begin();
  bas.begin();
  Bluefruit.Advertising.addService(svc); Bluefruit.Advertising.start(0);
}

void loop() {
  static uint32_t tImu = 0, tHap = 0; static uint16_t seq = 0;
  uint32_t now = millis();
  if (now - tImu >= 40) {              // 25 Hz stream
    tImu = now;
    int16_t p[4] = { (int16_t)(imu.readFloatAccelX()*8192), (int16_t)(imu.readFloatAccelY()*8192),
                     (int16_t)(imu.readFloatAccelZ()*8192), (int16_t)seq++ };
    if (Bluefruit.connected()) imuC.notify((uint8_t*)p, 8);
  }
  if (now - tHap >= 20) {              // 50 Hz envelope update
    tHap = now;
    drv.setRealtimeValue(amp);
  }
}
  • Why amplitude over BLE, not patterns? For v0 the phone streams the envelope at 50 Hz (one byte per write). It keeps all pattern logic in the app where you can iterate fast. Move patterns into firmware in v1 so the puck keeps pacing if the phone sleeps.
  • Auto-calibration. Run it once with the motor glued in its final cup, because mounting changes resonance. Store the result registers in flash if you want to skip it on boot.
  • Power. The nRF52840 with a 25 Hz stream averages under 2 mA. The motor at 40% average envelope draws about 30 mA. A 100 mAh cell gives roughly three to four hours of sessions.
  • Flashing. USB-C, double-tap reset for the bootloader, upload from Arduino IDE. No programmer needed.
Phone app

Where the product actually lives

SwiftUI with CoreBluetooth, or Flutter with flutter_blue_plus. Either way the app has four jobs: connect, detect breath, run a session, and remember it.

BLE contract

Service
custom 128-bit UUID, advertised
IMU
notify, 8 bytes: ax, ay, az as int16 at 8192 counts per g, plus a sequence number to spot dropped packets
Haptic
write without response, 1 byte amplitude at 50 Hz in v0; a 6-byte pattern struct in v1
Battery
standard Battery Service 0x180F

Screens for v0

  • Connect, with battery and "where is it clipped" readout

  • Session: pick a pattern, see the swell, feel it, timer
  • Summary: average bpm, time in target, a trace to scroll
Build the algorithm before the hardware arrives. Use CMMotionManager at 25 Hz with the phone lying on your chest. Same pipeline, same data shape. When the puck arrives you swap the data source and nothing else changes.
Build order

Four weeks, in order

  1. Day 1. Order everything in the core kit. Digi-Key ships next day; K&J and Adafruit in two to three days. Print the two wear-test dummies, glue magnets in, and wear them on a shirt for a day. This answers weight, magnet strength and the ribbon length before any electronics exist.
  2. Week 1. App first. Breath detection from the phone's own accelerometer on your chest. Target: your bpm count matches a manual count within one breath per minute over three minutes, seated. Build the session screen and the envelope generator while you are at it.
  3. Week 2. Desk prototype. Flash the firmware. Wire XIAO to DRV2605L to LRA on a desk with the LRA taped to a mint tin so you can feel it. Get a swell you like. Then stream IMU data to the app and swap the data source. Don't glue anything yet.
  4. Week 3. Into the shells. Print cups and lids, wire the ribbon, glue magnets and motor, run auto-calibration with the motor in place, close it up. Wear it for a real session.
  5. Week 4. Other people. Five friends, five-minute coherent session each, eyes closed. Count their breaths yourself. Ask one question afterwards: would you wear this again tomorrow? That answer decides whether v1 exists.

Tests that must fail when they should

A test that passes against broken code is worse than none. Each of these pairs a positive check with its inverse.

  • Breath detection: seated normal breathing shows 10 to 18 bpm; then hold your breath for 15 s and the trace must flatten and the bpm must drop out, not hold the last value.
  • Motion gate: stand up and walk; the app must say "hold still" within 2 s and must not report breaths while walking.
  • Off-body: puck on the desk shows confidence near zero and no breaths. If it detects breathing on a desk, your threshold is wrong.
  • Position detection: move the puck from neckline to sleeve; the app must switch to pace-only. Move it back; sensing resumes within 10 s.
  • Haptic phase: blindfolded tester follows the swell and reports inhale versus exhale; agreement must beat 90% over 20 breaths. Then invert the envelope in software and check they follow the inversion, proving they feel the device and not their own rhythm.
  • Dropped packets: walk the phone to another room until BLE drops; sequence gaps are logged and the trace shows a gap, not a straight line.
  • Battery: full charge, run coherent sessions back to back; log the time to shutdown. Expect three hours or more. Charger current measured at the USB port stays near 50 mA.
  • Magnets: jog in place for 30 s wearing it on a t-shirt; it must not slip more than a centimetre.

Claims. "Helps you slow your breathing" is a wellness product. "Treats anxiety" or "lowers blood pressure" is a medical device with regulatory consequences. Write the copy for v0 as the former and keep it there until you have a reason and a lawyer.

The non-part costs

  • Electrical engineer, contract: schematic and layout for the board above, two spins, about four to six weeks of work.
  • Certification: with a pre-certified module you test the finished product as an unintentional radiator: FCC Part 15B and CE EMC, typically $3k to $8k at a test lab. UN38.3 for the battery to ship by air, about $1k to $2k. Bluetooth SIG listing fee if you use the Bluetooth name and logo.
  • Industrial design: the ribbon, the finish, the dock. This is what makes it a product people photograph. Budget a few weeks of a designer's time after v0 proves the behaviour.
  • Stage 0 still applies. An Apple Watch version of the pacing and HRV features costs nothing in hardware and reaches people who will never clip a puck to a shirt. Ship it alongside.
